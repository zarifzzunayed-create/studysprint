# Part 1 — Know the test

## 1.1 What the section is

The SAT has two sections: **Reading and Writing** and **Math**. People often call Reading and Writing "the English section," but its official name is Reading and Writing, and it combines what used to be two separate sections (Reading and Writing and Language) into one.

It tests two broad abilities:

- **Reading:** understanding what a short text says, how it is built, what it implies, and what evidence supports a claim.
- **Writing:** editing a short text so it follows the conventions of standard written English (grammar and punctuation) and expresses ideas clearly (transitions and using notes to meet a goal).

You do not need outside knowledge of the topics. Every answer can be proven from the passage, the graph, or a grammar rule.

## 1.2 Format at a glance

| Feature | Reading and Writing | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Delivery | Digital, in College Board's Bluebook app | **[verify]** current delivery details |
| Modules | 2 modules | Module 2 adapts to how you did on Module 1 |
| Questions | 54 total: 27 per module | Includes a small number of unscored pretest questions **[verify]** (commonly cited as 4 for the section) |
| Time | 64 minutes total: 32 per module | **[verify]** |
| Average time per question | about 71 seconds | 64 × 60 ÷ 54 ≈ 71.1 s |
| Passage length | Short: about 25–150 words | **[verify]** range; one passage (or a pair) per question |
| Questions per passage | One | Unlike the old SAT's long passages with ~10 questions each |
| Answer format | All multiple choice, 4 options (A–D) | No student-produced answers in this section |
| Wrong-answer penalty | None | Always answer every question |
| Score | 200–800 | Combined with Math for a 400–1600 total **[verify]** |

### The four content domains

Every question belongs to one of four domains, each with specific skills. **[verify]** the percentages and question ranges.

| Domain | Share of section | Approx. questions | Skills tested |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Craft and Structure** | ~28% | 13–15 | Words in Context · Text Structure and Purpose · Cross-Text Connections |
| **Information and Ideas** | ~26% | 12–14 | Central Ideas and Details · Command of Evidence (Textual and Quantitative) · Inferences |
| **Standard English Conventions** | ~26% | 11–15 | Boundaries · Form, Structure, and Sense |
| **Expression of Ideas** | ~20% | 8–12 | Rhetorical Synthesis · Transitions |

**What this tells you about studying:** grammar plus transitions plus notes questions (Conventions and Expression of Ideas) make up close to half the section. Those are the most *learnable* points on the test: a fixed set of rules and patterns that, once mastered, can be answered fast and accurately. Most students should master them first.

## 1.3 How the adaptive modules work

The digital SAT is **multistage adaptive** at the module level, not question by question.

1. **Module 1** contains a mix of easy, medium, and hard questions. Everyone gets a comparable mix.
2. Based on your performance in Module 1, **Module 2** is either a **harder** version or a **less difficult** version.
3. Your score depends on which questions you answer correctly, *and* on how difficult those questions are. Reaching the harder Module 2 is necessary for the highest scores. **[verify]** College Board's current description of this.

**What this means for you:**

- **Module 1 matters a lot.** Careless errors there can route you to the easier Module 2, which limits how high you can score. Be accurate in Module 1.
- **A hard-feeling Module 2 is usually good news.** If Module 2 feels much harder than Module 1, you were probably routed to the harder module. Don't panic.
- **You can't go back.** Once a module ends, you can't return to it. Within a module, you can move freely, skip, and come back.
- **You won't know which questions are unscored.** Pretest questions look like all the others, so treat every question as if it counts.

## 1.4 What the questions look like and how they are ordered

Each question has:

- a **short passage** (or two short passages, labeled Text 1 and Text 2, or a passage with a table or graph);
- a **question** (sometimes called the stem);
- **four answer choices**.

Many writing questions have a **blank** in the passage; you choose what goes in it. Some reading questions **underline** one sentence and ask about its function.

**Order within each module [verify]:** questions are grouped by skill type, so similar questions come together. They commonly appear in this order:

1. Craft and Structure (Words in Context, then Text Structure and Purpose, then Cross-Text Connections)
2. Information and Ideas (Central Ideas, Command of Evidence, Inferences)
3. Standard English Conventions (Boundaries; Form, Structure, and Sense)
4. Expression of Ideas (Transitions, then Rhetorical Synthesis)

Within each group, questions are generally arranged from easier to harder **[verify]**. Practically, the first questions of a new group are usually faster, and the last ones in a group deserve more care.

**Why the order matters for timing:** the grammar block near the end of each module can be done quickly once you know the rules. Students who spend too long early on Words in Context often rush the grammar, which is the most learnable part of the test. See [Part 9](#part-9--timing-and-test-day-strategy).

## 1.5 Scoring basics

- Each section is scored **200–800**; the total is **400–1600**. **[verify]**
- There is **no penalty for wrong answers**. A blank and a wrong answer score the same, so never leave a question blank.
- Raw-score-to-scaled-score conversions are not published as a fixed table for the adaptive test, and they vary with question difficulty. **Do not publish score predictions** on StudySprint from short practice sets; they would not be reliable.

## 1.7 If you studied for the old paper SAT

| Old paper SAT | Digital SAT |
|---|---|
| Separate Reading and Writing and Language sections | One Reading and Writing section |
| Long passages (500–750 words) with about 10–11 questions each | Short passages (about 25–150 words), one question each |
| Paired "evidence" questions ("Which lines support the previous answer?") | Gone |
| Fixed test for everyone | Adaptive second module |
| Longer test | Shorter overall |

Old long-passage strategies (skimming, line-reference maps, pacing across passages) mostly don't apply. What does carry over: reading carefully for evidence, grammar rules, transition logic, and trap-answer awareness.

# Part 2 — The universal method

## 2.1 The four-step approach for every question

**Step 1: Read the question first.** Know your job before you read. A Words in Context question means you focus on the blank. An evidence question means you find and highlight the claim. A grammar question means you go straight to the blank and its punctuation.

**Step 2: Read the passage actively.** For reading questions, read the whole passage; it's short. Ask: *What is the main point? What is the author doing here? Where is the blank, and what must it connect to?*

**Step 3: Predict before you look at the choices.** Say your own answer in a few words: a word for a blank, a function ("introduces a counterexample"), a claim, a relationship ("contrast"). Predicting protects you from attractive wrong answers.

**Step 4: Eliminate, then choose.** Compare each choice to your prediction. Cross out every choice you can disprove with the text. If two remain, find the exact word that makes one of them wrong.

## 2.2 The proof rule

> **The right answer is the one you can prove with the text. Every wrong answer has something you can point to that makes it wrong.**

This is the single most important idea for the reading questions. The test is not asking what is *plausible*, *interesting*, or *true in the real world*. It's asking what the passage *supports*. If you can't point to the words that justify an answer, it isn't the answer.

On grammar questions, the proof is a **rule**: you should be able to name the rule that makes the right answer right and the others wrong.

## 2.3 The six trap answers

| Trap | What it looks like | How to catch it |
|---|---|---|
| **Too extreme** | Uses absolute words (*always, never, all, proves, completely, only*) when the passage is careful | Check the passage's strength: *suggests* ≠ *proves*; *some* ≠ *all* |
| **Out of scope** | Brings in something the passage never discusses | Ask: "Where does the passage say this?" |
| **Half right** | Starts accurately, then adds one wrong detail | Read every word of the choice; one wrong word makes it wrong |
| **True but irrelevant** | A true statement about the passage or graph that doesn't answer the question | Re-read the question: does this do *the specific job* asked? |
| **Opposite** | Reverses the passage's relationship or the author's stance | Watch for *not*, *despite*, *however* in the passage |
| **Recycled language** | Repeats exact words from the passage but distorts their meaning | Familiar words are not evidence; check what the choice actually claims |

**Two more habits:** (1) the right answer often *paraphrases* the passage rather than quoting it, and (2) the "most precise" or "most logical" answer beats one that is merely acceptable.

## 2.4 Reading short passages well

- **Find the pivot.** Many passages turn on a contrast word: *however, but, yet, although, despite, in contrast*. The idea after the pivot is usually the author's real point.
- **Separate voices.** Passages often report what "some researchers argue" before giving the author's view. Keep track of *who* believes *what*.
- **Note the strength of claims.** *May, suggests, likely, some* are cautious; *shows, demonstrates, all* are strong. Answer choices must match that strength.
- **Read the blank's neighbors.** For a blank, the sentence before it and the sentence after it usually contain the clue.
- **Don't use outside knowledge.** Even if you know a lot about the topic, answer only from the text.

# Part 3 — Craft and Structure

## 3.1 Words in Context

**What it asks:** which word or phrase best fits a blank, or what a word in the text most nearly means.

**Common stems:**
- "Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?"
- "As used in the text, what does the word ___ most nearly mean?"

**Method:**
1. Cover the choices. Read the sentence and find the **clue**: a definition, an example, a contrast, or a cause.
2. **Predict your own word** for the blank. A rough word is fine ("careful," "surprising," "weakened").
3. Match your prediction to the choices. Choose the word that fits the *meaning* and the *tone*.
4. Plug your choice back in and reread the full sentence.

**Clue types to look for:**

| Clue type | Signal words | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Definition or restatement | *that is, in other words, colon (:)* | "She was ___: she checked every result three times." → careful |
| Contrast | *but, however, although, despite, rather than* | "Rather than ___ the plan, the council approved it." → rejecting |
| Example | *such as, for example, including* | "___ materials such as glass and porcelain" → fragile/brittle |
| Cause or effect | *because, so, therefore, as a result* | "Because the data were ___, no conclusion was possible." → incomplete/inconclusive |

**Traps:**
- **The common meaning trap.** The test often uses a familiar word in a less common sense: *qualified* (limited), *check* (restrain), *sanction* (approve or penalize), *arrest* (stop), *appreciate* (increase in value), *temper* (moderate). Always choose the meaning the context requires.
- **Right topic, wrong precision.** Several choices may relate to the topic; only one is *precise*.
- **Tone mismatch.** A positive context needs a positive word.

> ### Example (original)
> Marine biologist Lena Okafor's study of coral reef recovery was notably ______: rather than drawing conclusions from a single season of observations, she returned to the same reef sites every year for over a decade before publishing her findings.
>
> **Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?**
> A) hasty
> B) painstaking
> C) controversial
> D) theoretical
>
> **Answer: B.** The colon introduces a restatement: returning to the same sites for over a decade instead of relying on one season describes very careful, thorough work, which is "painstaking."
> - **A** is the opposite: a hasty study would draw conclusions quickly.
> - **C** isn't supported: nothing suggests disagreement about the study.
> - **D** contradicts the text: she gathered years of field observations, the opposite of purely theoretical work.

## 3.2 Text Structure and Purpose

**What it asks:** what the passage as a whole does, what one underlined sentence does, or how the passage is organized.

**Common stems:**
- "Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?"
- "Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?"
- "Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?"

**Method:**
1. Summarize what each sentence *does*, not what it *says*: "introduces a belief," "gives an example," "challenges the belief," "explains a cause."
2. For an underlined sentence, ask how it relates to the sentences **before and after** it.
3. Match the **verbs** in the answer choices carefully. "Describes," "argues," "challenges," "illustrates," "explains," "compares," and "criticizes" mean different things.

**Common structures:**
- A common belief → evidence that challenges it
- A problem → a proposed solution
- A claim → an example that supports it
- A phenomenon → an explanation of its cause
- A description of a character → a detail that reveals their feelings (literature)

**Traps:**
- **Wrong verb.** "Criticizes" when the author only describes; "proves" when the author only suggests.
- **Too narrow.** Describes only one sentence when the question asks about the whole text.
- **Content without function.** Accurately restates what the sentence says but not *what it does* in the passage.

> ### Example (original)
> Many people picture deserts as nearly lifeless places. The Sonoran Desert, however, supports a remarkable variety of plant life, from towering saguaro cacti to small annual wildflowers. Many of those wildflowers spend most years as seeds resting in the soil, sprouting and blooming only after the desert's infrequent rains.
>
> **Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?**
> A) It presents a common belief and then offers details that challenge it.
> B) It describes a process and then explains each of its steps in order.
> C) It compares two deserts and explains why one has more plant life.
> D) It states a hypothesis and then describes an experiment that tests it.
>
> **Answer: A.** The first sentence gives a common view (deserts are lifeless), "however" marks the turn, and the rest gives evidence of rich plant life that challenges it.
> - **B:** the passage mentions how wildflowers sprout after rain, but the text as a whole isn't organized as a step-by-step process.
> - **C:** only one desert is discussed.
> - **D:** there is no hypothesis or experiment.

## 3.3 Cross-Text Connections

**What it asks:** how two short texts (Text 1 and Text 2) relate, usually how the author of one would respond to the other.

**Common stems:**
- "Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the claim in Text 1?"
- "Based on the texts, both authors would most likely agree that…"

**Method:**
1. After Text 1, write a 3–5 word summary of its **main claim**.
2. After Text 2, write its main claim, and decide its stance toward Text 1: **agrees, disagrees, partly agrees (qualifies), or adds a new consideration**.
3. Predict the response before reading the choices.

**Key insight:** Text 2 often **partly** agrees, accepting a fact from Text 1 but disputing its interpretation or its scope. Choices that say "completely rejects" are often too extreme.

**Traps:**
- Mixing up which author said what.
- Making the disagreement too extreme or too broad.
- Answering about one text only when the question asks about both.

> ### Example (original)
> **Text 1:** Some historians argue that the printing press transformed European society almost immediately. As evidence, they point to how quickly printed books spread across Europe in the decades after the 1450s.
>
> **Text 2:** Printed books did spread quickly, but reading them was another matter. Literacy rose slowly, and books remained too expensive for many people for generations. The press's deepest effects on everyday life unfolded over centuries, not decades.
>
> **Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the historians discussed in Text 1?**
> A) By agreeing that printed books spread quickly but arguing that the press's broad social effects took much longer to develop
> B) By disputing the claim that printed books spread across Europe after the 1450s
> C) By arguing that the printing press had little effect on European society
> D) By claiming that literacy rates rose faster than book production did
>
> **Answer: A.** Text 2 accepts the historians' evidence ("did spread quickly") but rejects their conclusion about timing ("centuries, not decades"). That's a partial agreement.
> - **B** is the opposite: Text 2 agrees the books spread quickly.
> - **C** is too extreme: Text 2 says the effects were slow and "deepest," not small.
> - **D** reverses Text 2, which says literacy rose slowly.

# Part 4 — Information and Ideas

## 4.1 Central Ideas and Details

**What it asks:** the main idea of a passage, or a specific detail it states.

**Common stems:**
- "Which choice best states the main idea of the text?"
- "According to the text, what is true about…?"
- "Based on the text, what is [character] doing in this passage?" (literature)

**Method:**
1. After reading, state the main idea in **one sentence** of your own.
2. Check that it covers the **whole** passage, not just one detail.
3. For detail questions, find the exact sentence that answers the question; the right choice paraphrases it.

**Traps:** too narrow (one detail), too broad (bigger than the passage), too extreme, and true-but-not-the-point.

> ### Example (original)
> Urban planner Dario Mendes studied several neighborhoods that converted street parking spaces into small public parks. Before the changes, some shop owners predicted they would lose customers without nearby parking. In nearly every neighborhood Mendes studied, however, businesses on the affected streets reported steady or even increased sales in the following year.
>
> **Which choice best states the main idea of the text?**
> A) Converting parking spaces into parks did not hurt, and may have helped, nearby businesses, despite some owners' predictions.
> B) Shop owners generally oppose changes to their neighborhoods.
> C) Street parking is more valuable to cities than public parks are.
> D) Mendes argues that every city should replace its street parking with parks.
>
> **Answer: A.** It captures both the prediction and the contradicting result, with appropriately careful wording ("may have helped").
> - **B** is too broad: the passage mentions only "some" owners and one kind of change.
> - **C** is the opposite of what the findings suggest.
> - **D** is too extreme and out of scope: the text reports findings, not a recommendation for every city.

## 4.2 Command of Evidence: Textual

**What it asks:** which evidence would **support** or **weaken** a claim, or which quotation **illustrates** a claim about a literary work.

**Common stems:**
- "Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researcher's hypothesis?"
- "Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken the student's claim?"
- "Which quotation from the poem most effectively illustrates the claim?"

**Method:**
1. **Find and highlight the exact claim or hypothesis.** This is the most important step.
2. Break the claim into its parts. A claim like "X happens *because of* Y" has two parts: X happens, and Y causes it.
3. Predict what kind of evidence would prove (or disprove) **all** the parts.
4. Choose the option that most **directly** addresses the claim's key relationship.

**"If true":** the phrase "if true" means you accept the finding as fact. Don't judge whether it's realistic; judge whether it *bears on the claim*.

**Traps:**
- Evidence about the **right topic** that doesn't test the specific **claim**.
- Evidence that supports only **half** the claim.
- For quotation questions: a quotation that mentions the right subject but doesn't show the **specific quality** in the claim (for example, the claim says the speaker feels *regret*, but the quotation only describes the setting).

> ### Example (original)
> Biologist Hana Ito hypothesizes that a particular desert beetle gets drinking water from fog: when fog rolls in, the beetle tilts its body so that water droplets collect on its back and run down toward its mouth.
>
> **Which finding, if true, would most directly support Ito's hypothesis?**
> A) During fog, the beetles tilt their bodies head-down, and droplets that form on their backs flow toward their mouths.
> B) The beetles are most active during the cool hours of the night.
> C) Beetles kept in a laboratory drink readily from shallow dishes of water.
> D) Fog occurs frequently in the region where the beetles live.
>
> **Answer: A.** The hypothesis has two parts: the tilting behavior and droplets reaching the mouth. Choice A confirms both.
> - **B** concerns activity patterns, not water collection.
> - **C** shows that beetles drink water, but not that they collect it from fog.
> - **D** is a necessary condition, but it doesn't show the beetles collect fog water.

## 4.3 Command of Evidence: Quantitative

**What it asks:** which choice uses data from a **table or graph** to complete or support a statement in the passage.

**Common stem:** "Which choice most effectively uses data from the table/graph to complete the text?"

**Method:**
1. Read the passage first and identify **what the blank must show**: usually a specific comparison or trend that supports a claim.
2. Then read the table or graph: title, axes, units, categories, and legend.
3. Check each choice for **two things**: (a) is it **accurate** according to the data? (b) does it **support the specific point** in the passage?
4. Eliminate choices that are accurate but don't make the point.

**Key insight:** On these questions, often **three or even all four choices are accurate** descriptions of the data. Only one does the *job*. The passage, not the graph, decides what job is needed.

**Data-reading checklist:**
- Units (thousands? percentages? per year?)
- Which variable is on which axis
- Whether the question is about a **difference**, a **trend**, a **maximum or minimum**, or a **comparison between groups**
- Whether the claim is about **one group** or a **comparison** between groups

> ### Example (original)
> **Average daily visitors, Eastside Library branch**
>
> | Month | Average daily visitors |
> |---|---|
> | April | 410 |
> | May | 420 |
> | June | 560 |
> | July | 590 |
>
> In June, the Eastside branch began opening on Sundays. Librarian Ava Ruiz believes the change attracted more visitors to the branch, pointing out that ______
>
> **Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the statement?**
> A) average daily visitors rose from 420 in May to 560 in June and 590 in July.
> B) the branch averaged 410 daily visitors in April.
> C) average daily visitors were higher in July than in June.
> D) average daily visitors were nearly the same in April and May.
>
> **Answer: A.** The claim is that a change starting in June increased visits, so the evidence must compare *before* (May) with *after* (June and July). Only A makes that comparison.
> - **B, C,** and **D** are all accurate but don't make the point.
> - **B** describes one month only.
> - **C** compares two months that are both *after* the change.
> - **D** describes the period before the change only.

## 4.4 Inferences

**What it asks:** what **logically follows** from the passage, usually by completing its final sentence.

**Common stem:** "Which choice most logically completes the text?"

**Method:**
1. Read the passage as an **argument**: what facts are given, and what pattern or tension do they create?
2. Predict the conclusion that **must** follow, or most reasonably follows, using *only* the passage.
3. Choose the most **modest** choice that fully accounts for the facts. Inference answers are usually cautious.

**Key insight:** an inference is not a guess. It is the conclusion the passage *sets up*. Look for contrasts in the passage ("declined in A, but not in B"). The answer usually **explains the difference**.

**Traps:**
- A conclusion that's plausible in real life but not supported by the passage.
- A conclusion that goes further than the evidence ("proves that…", "always…").
- A choice that contradicts one of the given facts.

> ### Example (original)
> Ecologists studying a lake found that a population of small fish declined sharply in the years after a larger predatory fish was introduced. The decline, however, happened only in the lake's open water. In shallow areas filled with dense weeds, the small fish remained as abundant as before. This pattern suggests that ______
>
> **Which choice most logically completes the text?**
> A) the dense weeds may provide the small fish with shelter from the predator.
> B) the predatory fish prefer to hunt in shallow, weedy water.
> C) the small fish declined mainly because of pollution in the open water.
> D) the weeds harm the predatory fish.
>
> **Answer: A.** The key contrast is decline in open water and no decline among the weeds after the predator arrived. A explains that difference, cautiously ("may").
> - **B** works against the data: if the predator hunted mostly in the weeds, the small fish there would have declined.
> - **C** brings in a cause the passage never mentions, and it ignores the timing after the predator arrived.
> - **D** isn't supported: nothing suggests the weeds affect the predator itself.

# Part 5 — Standard English Conventions (the grammar rulebook)

**What it asks:** which choice makes the sentence conform to the conventions of standard English.

**Common stem:** "Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?"

**Two big skill groups:**
- **Boundaries:** punctuation within and between sentences (commas, semicolons, colons, dashes, periods).
- **Form, Structure, and Sense:** agreement, verb forms, pronouns, modifiers, plurals, and possessives.

**The good news:** these questions test a **limited, predictable set of rules**. Learn the rules below, and these become the fastest points on the test.

**How to spot what's being tested:** look at what **changes** between the four choices.
- Only punctuation changes → a Boundaries rule.
- The verb form changes → agreement, tense, or finite vs. nonfinite verb.
- Pronouns change → pronoun agreement or *its/it's*.
- The word order of a whole phrase changes → modifier placement.
- Apostrophes and *-s* endings change → plurals and possessives.

## 5.1 Sentence anatomy: the only theory you need

| Term | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Independent clause** | Has a subject and a main (finite) verb and can stand alone as a sentence | *The bridge opened in 1932.* |
| **Dependent clause** | Has a subject and verb but begins with a subordinating word, so it can't stand alone | *because the bridge opened in 1932* |
| **Phrase** | A group of words without a subject–verb pair | *after years of construction* |
| **Subordinating words** | Turn an independent clause into a dependent one | *because, although, while, when, if, since, unless, after, before, whereas, which, who, that* |
| **Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS)** | *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so* | Join independent clauses with a comma before them |
| **Conjunctive adverbs (transition words)** | *however, therefore, moreover, thus, consequently, nevertheless, furthermore, instead* | Do **not** join clauses on their own; they need a period or semicolon before them |

**The core skill:** for any blank, ask, "Is there an independent clause on the left? Is there one on the right?" Most Boundaries questions are solved by that question.

## 5.2 Boundaries: joining clauses

### The joining table

| To join two independent clauses, use… | Example | Correct? |
|---|---|---|
| A **period** | The museum reopened. Visitors returned. | ✅ |
| A **semicolon** | The museum reopened; visitors returned. | ✅ |
| A **comma + FANBOYS** | The museum reopened, and visitors returned. | ✅ |
| A **colon**, if the second explains or elaborates the first | The museum faced one problem: few visitors returned. | ✅ |
| A **single dash**, for emphasis or explanation (works like a colon) | The museum faced a surprise—visitors returned in record numbers. | ✅ |
| A **comma alone** | The museum reopened, visitors returned. | ❌ comma splice |
| **Nothing** | The museum reopened visitors returned. | ❌ run-on |
| A **comma + transition word** | The museum reopened, however, visitors returned. | ❌ comma splice |
| **Semicolon + transition + comma** | The museum reopened; however, visitors returned slowly. | ✅ |

### The three most tested patterns

**1. Comma splice.** Two independent clauses joined only by a comma.
- ❌ *The experiment failed, the team tried again.*
- ✅ *The experiment failed; the team tried again.*
- ✅ *The experiment failed, so the team tried again.*

**2. Transition words between clauses.** *However, therefore, moreover,* etc. can't join clauses by themselves.
- ❌ *The results were promising, however, more tests are needed.*
- ✅ *The results were promising; however, more tests are needed.*
- ✅ *The results were promising. However, more tests are needed.*
- Inside a single clause, a transition is set off by commas: *The results, however, need more testing.* ✅

**3. Semicolon misuse.** A semicolon needs an **independent clause on both sides**.
- ❌ *Although the results were promising; more tests are needed.* (The left side is dependent.)
- ✅ *Although the results were promising, more tests are needed.*

### Fragments

A fragment is a group of words punctuated as a sentence that lacks an independent clause.
- ❌ *The painting, which hung in the gallery for decades.* (No main verb for "painting.")
- ✅ *The painting, which hung in the gallery for decades, was restored last year.*

### Dependent clauses and commas
- Dependent clause **first** → comma: *Because it rained, the game was canceled.* ✅
- Dependent clause **second** → usually no comma: *The game was canceled because it rained.* ✅

> ### Example (original)
> The first transcontinental railroad in the United States was completed in 1869 ______ it cut the journey from coast to coast from several months to about a week.
>
> A) 1869, it
> B) 1869; it
> C) 1869 it
> D) 1869, and, it
>
> **Answer: B.** Both sides are independent clauses, and a semicolon correctly joins them.
> - **A** is a comma splice.
> - **C** is a run-on.
> - **D** puts an extra comma after "and," which incorrectly separates the conjunction from the clause it introduces.

## 5.3 Boundaries: essential vs. nonessential elements

**Nonessential (nonrestrictive) information** adds extra detail; the sentence is complete and its meaning is unchanged without it. Set it off with a **matching pair**: two commas, two dashes, or two parentheses.

- ✅ *The Golden Gate Bridge, which opened in 1937, is in San Francisco.*
- ✅ *The Golden Gate Bridge—which opened in 1937—is in San Francisco.*
- ❌ *The Golden Gate Bridge, which opened in 1937—is in San Francisco.* (mismatched pair)
- ❌ *The Golden Gate Bridge, which opened in 1937 is in San Francisco.* (missing the second comma)

**The "remove it" test:** delete the words between the punctuation marks. If what's left is a complete, correct sentence with the same core meaning, the element is nonessential and needs a matching pair.

**Essential (restrictive) information** identifies *which one* you mean. **No commas.**
- ✅ *Students who finish early may leave.* (Which students? The ones who finish early.)
- ❌ *Students, who finish early, may leave.* (This wrongly says all students finish early.)

**Which vs. that:** *that* introduces essential clauses with no commas. *Which* usually introduces nonessential clauses with commas.

**The two-comma trap:** the SAT often gives you the first comma of a nonessential element in the passage and asks you to supply the second. Look back for an opening comma or dash, and close it with the **same** mark.

> ### Example (original)
> Mount Rainier ______ is the tallest mountain in Washington State.
>
> A) an active volcano, southeast of Seattle
> B) , an active volcano southeast of Seattle,
> C) , an active volcano southeast of Seattle—
> D) an active volcano southeast of Seattle,
>
> **Answer: B.** "An active volcano southeast of Seattle" is a nonessential appositive. Remove it and the sentence still works ("Mount Rainier is the tallest mountain…"). It needs a matching pair of commas.
> - **A** and **D** each leave the element with a single comma, so it isn't properly set off.
> - **C** mixes a comma and a dash.

## 5.4 Boundaries: colons and dashes

### Colons
A colon must follow a **complete independent clause**. What comes after can be a list, an explanation, an example, or a quotation.
- ✅ *The recipe needs three things: flour, water, and salt.*
- ✅ *She had one goal: to finish the marathon.*
- ❌ *The recipe needs: flour, water, and salt.* (The left side isn't complete; a colon can't come between a verb and its object.)
- ❌ *The recipe includes ingredients such as: flour and salt.* (Never put a colon after *such as*, *including*, or *for example*.)

**Colon or semicolon?** A semicolon joins two related independent clauses as equals. A colon says, "what follows explains or delivers what I just set up." The words after a colon don't need to be an independent clause; after a semicolon, they must be.

### Dashes
- A **pair** of dashes sets off nonessential information, like a pair of commas.
- A **single** dash can introduce an explanation, a list, or a dramatic turn at the end of a sentence, much like a colon. It also needs a complete clause before it.

## 5.5 Boundaries: when NOT to use a comma

Many wrong answers add commas where none belong. **Do not put a single comma:**

| Between… | ❌ Wrong | ✅ Right |
|---|---|---|
| A subject and its verb | *The scientist who led the study, published it.* | *The scientist who led the study published it.* |
| A verb and its object | *The study found, that sleep matters.* | *The study found that sleep matters.* |
| A preposition and its object | *The results of, the survey* | *The results of the survey* |
| Two verbs sharing one subject (compound predicate) | *She wrote the report, and submitted it.* | *She wrote the report and submitted it.* |
| An adjective and its noun | *a bright, star* | *a bright star* |
| Before an essential clause | *the book, that I borrowed* | *the book that I borrowed* |

**Rule of thumb:** when in doubt, a choice with **fewer** commas is often right on comma questions, but only if it doesn't create a splice or break a nonessential pair.

## 5.6 Boundaries: lists, introductions, and titles before names

**Lists:**
- Separate three or more items with commas: *She studied biology, chemistry, and physics.*
- If the items themselves contain commas, separate them with **semicolons**: *The tour visited Austin, Texas; Denver, Colorado; and Boise, Idaho.*

**Introductory elements:** put a comma after an introductory phrase or clause: *After the storm passed, crews began repairs.*

**Titles and descriptions before a name (a very common SAT pattern):** when a descriptive title comes directly before a name *without "the" or "a"*, **don't** separate them with a comma.
- ✅ *Astronomer Vera Rubin studied galaxy rotation.*
- ❌ *Astronomer, Vera Rubin, studied galaxy rotation.*
- ✅ *The novel's author, Chinua Achebe, was born in Nigeria.* (Here the name renames "the novel's author," which already identifies one person, so it's nonessential.)

**Quotations:** a short quotation that is part of the sentence's grammar needs no comma: *She called the results "promising."* A comma normally introduces a quotation after a verb of saying: *She said, "The results are promising."*

## 5.7 Form, Structure, and Sense: subject–verb agreement

**Rule:** a verb must agree in number with its **subject**, not with the nearest noun.

**Find the true subject:**
1. Cross out prepositional phrases (*of the…, in the…, with the…*).
2. Cross out nonessential elements between commas or dashes.
3. Ask: who or what is doing the verb?

| Situation | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Prepositional phrase between subject and verb | Ignore the phrase | *The **collection** of rare stamps **is** valuable.* |
| Compound subject with *and* | Plural | *The **teacher and** her **students** **are** traveling.* |
| Subjects joined by *or*/*nor* | Agree with the **nearer** subject | *Neither the players nor the **coach** **was** ready.* |
| *Each, every, either, neither, one, everyone, nobody* | Singular | ***Each** of the samples **was** tested.* |
| Inverted order (verb before subject) | Find the subject after the verb | *Among the findings **was** a **fossil**.* / *Among the findings **were** two **fossils**.* |
| Collective nouns (*team, committee, jury, company*) | Usually singular in American English | *The **committee** **has** decided.* |
| *The number of* vs. *a number of* | *The number* = singular; *a number* = plural | ***The number** of applicants **has** grown.* / ***A number** of applicants **have** called.* |
| Gerund (-ing) subject | Singular | ***Reading** old letters **reveals** a lot.* |

**Typical SAT trap:** a long, plural-sounding phrase between a singular subject and the blank verb.

> ### Example (original)
> The network of underground tunnels beneath the old city ______ mapped for the first time in 2019.
>
> A) were
> B) are
> C) was
> D) have been
>
> **Answer: C.** The subject is "network" (singular). "Of underground tunnels beneath the old city" is a prepositional phrase. The year 2019 calls for past tense: "was mapped."
> - **A** and **D** are plural.
> - **B** is plural and present tense.

## 5.8 Pronouns

**Pronoun–antecedent agreement:** a pronoun must match the noun it refers to in number.
- A singular thing, organization, or group → *it, its*: *The company announced **its** new policy.*
- Plural nouns → *they, them, their*: *The researchers published **their** results.*

**Most tested confusions:**

| Word | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **its** | belonging to it (possessive, **no apostrophe**) | *The tree lost its leaves.* |
| **it's** | it is / it has | *It's raining.* |
| **their** | belonging to them | *their findings* |
| **there** | a place, or "there is/are" | *There are three options.* |
| **they're** | they are | *They're arriving soon.* |
| **whose** | belonging to whom | *the author whose book won* |
| **who's** | who is / who has | *Who's presenting?* |

**Ambiguity:** a pronoun must clearly refer to **one** noun. If *it* or *they* could mean two different things, the answer that names the noun is better.

**Case:** use subject forms (*I, he, she, we, they, who*) as subjects and object forms (*me, him, her, us, them, whom*) as objects, including after prepositions: *between you and **me**.*

## 5.9 Verbs: tense and finite verbs

### Tense
- Match the **time frame** of the passage. If the surrounding sentences describe a past event, use past tense.
- Use the **past perfect** (*had studied*) for an action completed before another past action: *By the time the rescue team arrived, the storm **had passed**.*
- Use the **present perfect** (*has studied*) for something that started in the past and continues or matters now: *Scientists **have studied** the comet since 1986.*
- Use the **present tense** for general truths and for describing what happens in a book, poem, or artwork: *In the novel, the narrator **returns** to her hometown.*
- **Clue words** decide tense: *in 1890, last year, currently, since, by the time, for decades.*

### Finite vs. nonfinite verbs (a very common SAT test)
Every sentence needs a **finite** (main) verb, one that shows tense. Forms ending in *-ing*, *to* + verb, and *having* + verb are **not** main verbs on their own.

- ❌ *The archaeologists, after years of searching, **discovering** the tomb in 1922.* (fragment)
- ✅ *The archaeologists, after years of searching, **discovered** the tomb in 1922.*

**How to use this:** when the choices are *discovered / discovering / to discover / having discovered*, check whether the sentence already has a main verb. If it doesn't, you need the finite form (*discovered*). If it does, you usually need the nonfinite form to attach a phrase.

> ### Example (original)
> After studying soil samples from hundreds of farms, agronomist Priya Raman ______ that crop rotation improved soil health more than any single fertilizer she tested.
>
> A) concluding
> B) to conclude
> C) concluded
> D) having concluded
>
> **Answer: C.** The sentence needs a main verb for the subject "agronomist Priya Raman," and "concluded" is the only finite form.
> - **A, B,** and **D** are nonfinite, so each one leaves the sentence without a main verb, which makes it a fragment.

## 5.10 Modifiers

**Rule:** a modifying phrase must sit right next to the thing it describes. The most tested form is the **introductory modifier**: the phrase before the comma must describe the **subject that comes right after the comma**.

- ❌ *Having studied the data for months, **the conclusion** seemed obvious.* (The conclusion didn't study the data.)
- ✅ *Having studied the data for months, **the researchers** found the conclusion obvious.*

**How to solve it:** ask, "**Who or what** is doing the action in the opening phrase?" The right choice starts the main clause with that person or thing.

> ### Example (original)
> Built entirely without mortar, ______
>
> A) visitors are often amazed that the ancient stone wall still stands.
> B) the ancient stone wall still stands, often amazing visitors.
> C) it is amazing to visitors that the ancient stone wall still stands.
> D) the visitors' amazement comes from how the ancient stone wall still stands.
>
> **Answer: B.** "Built entirely without mortar" describes the wall, so "the wall" must come right after the comma.
> - **A, C,** and **D** each start with visitors, "it," or "the visitors' amazement," none of which was built without mortar.

## 5.11 Plurals and possessives

| Form | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Plural (more than one) | Add *-s*, **no apostrophe** | *the scientists* |
| Singular possessive | Add *'s* | *the scientist's notebook* (one scientist) |
| Plural possessive (ending in *s*) | Add an apostrophe after the *s* | *the scientists' notebooks* (several scientists) |
| Irregular plural possessive | Add *'s* | *the children's books, the women's team* |
| *It* possessive | **No apostrophe** | *its*, never *it's*, for possession |

**Method:**
1. Is it one or more than one? (Singular or plural?)
2. Does it own something that comes right after it? (Possessive or not?)

> ### Example (original)
> The three ______ new exhibit opened to the public last spring, featuring fossils collected over twenty years of fieldwork.
>
> A) museums
> B) museum's
> C) museums'
> D) museums's
>
> **Answer: C.** "Three" means plural, and the exhibit belongs to them, so a plural possessive is needed: "museums'."
> - **A** is plural but not possessive.
> - **B** is a singular possessive.
> - **D** isn't a standard form.

## 5.12 The conventions decision checklist

Use this order on every grammar question:

1. **What changes between the choices?** That tells you which rule is being tested.
2. **Punctuation question?** Mark the clause on each side of the blank: independent or not?
   - Both independent → period, semicolon, comma + FANBOYS, or (if it explains) colon/dash.
   - Not both independent → no semicolon, usually a comma or nothing.
3. **Is there an opening comma or dash earlier?** Close it with the same mark.
4. **Verb question?** Find the true subject (agreement), the time clues (tense), and whether a main verb already exists (finite vs. nonfinite).
5. **Opening modifier?** The noun after the comma must be the thing being described.
6. **Apostrophes?** One or many? Owning something or not?
7. **Still tied?** Pick the choice that is shortest *and* grammatically complete. Extra words and extra commas are usually wrong.

# Part 6 — Expression of Ideas

## 6.1 Transitions

**What it asks:** which transition word or phrase logically connects two ideas.

**Common stem:** "Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?"

**Method:**
1. **Cover the choices.**
2. Read the sentence **before** the blank and the sentence **with** the blank.
3. Decide the **relationship** in one word: *contrast, cause, example, addition, sequence, similarity, clarification, conclusion, concession*.
4. Pick the choice that matches. If two choices mean essentially the same thing (for example, *Therefore* and *Thus*), neither is likely to be the answer, since each question has only one correct choice. Use that to eliminate.

### Transition categories

| Relationship | Transitions | Use when the second idea… |
|---|---|---|
| **Addition** | *Furthermore, Moreover, In addition, Additionally, Also* | adds a point in the same direction |
| **Contrast** | *However, By contrast, On the other hand, Nevertheless, Nonetheless, Still, Conversely* | conflicts with or differs from the first |
| **Replacement** | *Instead, Rather* | replaces an option that was rejected or didn't happen |
| **Concession** | *Admittedly, Granted, Of course* | concedes a point before the main argument continues |
| **Cause and effect** | *Therefore, Thus, Consequently, As a result, Hence, Accordingly* | is a result of the first |
| **Example** | *For example, For instance, Specifically* | gives a specific case of the first |
| **Clarification / restatement** | *In other words, That is* | restates the first more simply |
| **Emphasis / intensification** | *In fact, Indeed* | goes further than the first in the same direction |
| **Similarity** | *Similarly, Likewise, In the same way* | describes a parallel case |
| **Sequence / time** | *First, Then, Next, Subsequently, Later, Meanwhile, Finally, Previously* | follows (or precedes) in time |
| **Conclusion / summary** | *Ultimately, In sum, Overall, In conclusion* | sums up what came before |

**Subtle distinctions that the hardest questions test:**
- **However vs. Instead:** *however* marks a general contrast; *instead* means the second action **replaced** the first (*He planned to drive. Instead, he flew.*).
- **In fact vs. Moreover:** *in fact* intensifies or corrects (often going beyond the previous point); *moreover* simply adds.
- **Therefore vs. For example:** a result of the previous idea, vs. an instance of it.
- **Similarly vs. Likewise vs. In addition:** similarity is a parallel case in a *different* subject; addition is a further point about the *same* subject.
- **Meanwhile:** at the same time, often contrasting two situations happening at once.

> ### Example (original)
> Electric cars were common on American roads in the early 1900s. ______ cheaper gasoline-powered cars soon dominated the market, and electric models nearly disappeared for decades.
>
> A) For example,
> B) Similarly,
> C) However,
> D) As a result,
>
> **Answer: C.** The second sentence contrasts with the first: electric cars were common, but they then nearly disappeared.
> - **A:** gasoline cars dominating isn't an example of electric cars being common.
> - **B:** the two situations aren't parallel.
> - **D:** the decline wasn't caused by electric cars being common.

## 6.2 Rhetorical Synthesis (the "notes" questions)

**What it asks:** you're given a student's bullet-point notes and a **goal**. You choose the sentence that uses the notes to meet that goal.

**Common stem:** "While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes: [bullets]. The student wants to [goal]. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?"

**The secret:** **read the goal first, and read it carefully. The goal is the whole question.** You barely need the notes. Every choice is usually accurate to the notes; only one accomplishes the specific goal.

**Method:**
1. Read the **goal** sentence first. Highlight its key words: *emphasize a similarity, explain a difference, introduce the study to an audience unfamiliar with it, present the research's conclusion, specify the location*.
2. For each choice, ask: "Does this sentence do *exactly* that?"
3. Skim the notes only if you need to check accuracy.

**Goal key words and what they require:**

| Goal says… | The right answer must… |
|---|---|
| "emphasize a similarity" | state what two things have **in common** |
| "emphasize a difference / contrast" | state how two things **differ** (often uses *while, whereas, but, unlike*) |
| "introduce [X] to an audience unfamiliar with it" | include **who or what X is** (a brief description) |
| "present the study and its findings" | include **both** the study and the results |
| "explain the significance / impact" | state **why it matters** or its effect |
| "specify when/where" | include the **date** or **place** |
| "make and support a generalization" | state a general claim **and** give an example |

> ### Example (original)
> While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:
> - The Erie Canal is a waterway in New York State.
> - It connects the Hudson River to Lake Erie.
> - It opened in 1825 and is about 360 miles long.
> - It greatly lowered the cost of shipping goods between the Great Lakes region and New York City.
>
> The student wants to emphasize the canal's economic impact. **Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?**
>
> A) Opening in 1825, the Erie Canal greatly lowered the cost of shipping goods between the Great Lakes region and New York City.
> B) The Erie Canal is a waterway in New York State that is about 360 miles long.
> C) The Erie Canal connects the Hudson River to Lake Erie.
> D) The Erie Canal, which opened in 1825, runs from the Hudson River to Lake Erie.
>
> **Answer: A.** "Economic impact" means an effect on money or trade, and A is the only choice that mentions shipping costs.
> - **B, C,** and **D** are accurate to the notes but describe the canal's location, length, or date, not its economic impact.

# Part 7 — Passage genres

Passages are drawn from **literature, history and social studies, the humanities, and science**. Each genre has its own habits.

## Literature: prose fiction, poetry, and drama
- Passages can come from older works (for example, nineteenth- or early-twentieth-century fiction and poetry), so vocabulary and sentence structure may be unfamiliar. The question introduction usually tells you the author, title, and year; read it, because it gives you context.
- Focus on **character feelings, motives, and relationships**, and on **tone**.
- For **poems**: read them sentence by sentence, not line by line. Follow the punctuation, and ignore line breaks for meaning. Ask what changes from the beginning of the poem to the end.
- For **drama**: track who is speaking and what they want.
- Quotation-based evidence questions are common here: match the quotation to the **specific feeling or quality** in the claim.

## History and social studies
- Often present **arguments, policies, or historical documents**, sometimes with a contrasting view.
- Track **who holds which view**, and the **evidence** behind it.
- Historical documents may use formal, older language. Paraphrase each sentence in plain English.

## Humanities
- Topics include art, music, architecture, literature criticism, and philosophy.
- Often focus on **interpretations**: how a critic or scholar reads a work, and whether others agree.
- Watch for **qualified claims** ("partly," "in some respects").

## Science
- Usually describe a **study**: hypothesis, method, result, and interpretation.
- Many evidence and inference questions come from science passages.
- Separate **what was observed** from **what was concluded**, and notice how strongly the conclusion is stated.
- Don't be intimidated by technical terms. The passage gives you everything you need, and you rarely need to know what a technical term means to answer correctly.

# Part 8 — Vocabulary that actually matters

The digital SAT does **not** reward memorizing obscure words. It rewards knowing **academic words used precisely**, especially words with more than one meaning, and words that describe **tone, attitude, and argument**.

## How to build vocabulary efficiently
1. **Learn words in context.** Every time you miss a Words in Context question, add the correct word *and the wrong words you didn't know* to a list, with the sentence.
2. **Focus on secondary meanings** of common words (see below).
3. **Learn tone and argument words.** They appear in answer choices for purpose, structure, and cross-text questions.
4. **Review in short daily sessions** (10 minutes) rather than long cramming.

## Common words with tested secondary meanings

| Word | Common meaning | Secondary meaning to know |
|---|---|---|
| qualify | to meet requirements | to limit or modify a statement |
| check | to examine | to stop or restrain |
| sanction | — | can mean to *approve* or to *penalize* (context decides) |
| arrest | to take into custody | to stop or halt (a process) |
| appreciate | to be grateful for | to increase in value |
| temper | anger | to moderate or soften |
| novel | a book | new, original |
| grave | burial place | serious |
| conduct | behavior | to carry out; to transmit (heat, electricity) |
| command | an order | mastery (*a command of French*) |
| reservation | a booking | a doubt or hesitation |
| sound | a noise | reliable, well-founded (*a sound argument*) |
| account | a bank account | an explanation or description |
| assume | to suppose | to take on (*assume a role*) |
| figure | a number | a person, especially an important one |

## Tone and attitude words (common in answer choices)

| Positive | Neutral | Negative | Mixed / cautious |
|---|---|---|---|
| admiring | objective | dismissive | ambivalent (having mixed feelings) |
| enthusiastic | analytical | disdainful (showing contempt) | skeptical (doubtful) |
| reverent (deeply respectful) | descriptive | indignant (angry at unfairness) | wary |
| celebratory | informative | sardonic (mockingly cynical) | tentative (uncertain) |
| appreciative | matter-of-fact | condescending | nostalgic (wistful about the past) |

## Argument and analysis words

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **corroborate** | to confirm with more evidence |
| **undermine** | to weaken |
| **refute** | to prove wrong |
| **qualify** (a claim) | to limit it, or add conditions |
| **concede** | to admit a point, often reluctantly |
| **reconcile** | to make two ideas consistent |
| **speculate** | to form a theory without firm evidence |
| **posit** | to put forward as a basis for argument |
| **substantiate** | to support with evidence |
| **anomaly** | something that departs from the normal pattern |
| **empirical** | based on observation or experiment |
| **provisional** | temporary, subject to change |
| **ubiquitous** | found everywhere |
| **pragmatic** | practical, focused on results |
| **meticulous** | extremely careful and precise |
| **mitigate** | to make less severe |
| **exacerbate** | to make worse |
| **innocuous** | harmless |
| **ephemeral** | lasting a very short time |
| **unprecedented** | never done or known before |
| **conventional** | following accepted practice |
| **paradoxical** | seemingly self-contradictory |
| **juxtapose** | to place side by side for comparison |
| **cursory** | hasty and not thorough |
| **inadvertent** | not intentional |
| **arbitrary** | based on random choice, not reason |

# Part 9 — Timing and test-day strategy

## Pacing

You have about **71 seconds per question** on average, but questions are not equal:

| Question type | Target time |
|---|---|
| Conventions (grammar) | 30–50 seconds |
| Transitions | 30–45 seconds |
| Rhetorical Synthesis | 45–60 seconds |
| Words in Context | 45–75 seconds |
| Structure, Central Idea | 60–90 seconds |
| Evidence, Inference, Cross-Text | 75–120 seconds |

**Checkpoints for each 32-minute module:**
- After question 9: about 21 minutes should remain.
- After question 18: about 10–11 minutes should remain. (The faster grammar block is coming, so being slightly behind here is okay.)
- Leave 2–3 minutes at the end for your review pass.

## The two-minute rule
If you've spent about two minutes on a question and still can't decide:
1. Eliminate what you can.
2. Pick your best remaining answer.
3. Mark it for review and move on.

Never leave a question blank; there's no penalty for guessing.

## The review pass
With your remaining time:
1. Open the question navigator.
2. Go to marked questions first.
3. Check any blank questions (there shouldn't be any).
4. Don't change an answer without a specific reason you can point to in the text or a rule.

## Module 2 mindset
- If Module 2 feels hard, that's a good sign. Stay steady.
- If it feels easy, be extra careful; accuracy on every question matters.

## Test-day checklist [verify against College Board's current test-day rules]
- Bluebook installed and updated, and the device fully charged.
- Admission ticket and approved ID as required.
- Practice with Bluebook's tools in at least one full-length practice test beforehand.
- During the break, rest and eat; don't review notes in a way that raises anxiety.

# Part 10 — Study plans and the error log

## Step 1: Take a diagnostic
Take a **full-length official practice test in Bluebook** under real conditions. Then sort every missed or guessed question by **skill** (the ten skills in Part 1) and by **reason** (see the error log). Your weakest skills, weighted by how many questions they represent, decide your plan.

## Step 2: Keep an error log
For every missed **and every guessed** question, record:

| Date | Skill | What I chose | Correct answer | Why I missed it | Rule or lesson |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Why I missed it** should be one of:
- **Content gap:** I didn't know the rule or the word.
- **Misread:** I misread the question, passage, or graph.
- **Trap:** I fell for a trap type (name it).
- **Timing:** I rushed or ran out of time.
- **Overthinking:** I changed a correct answer, or argued myself out of it.

Review your log weekly. Patterns matter more than individual mistakes.

## Study plans

### 2-week plan (emergency)
- **Days 1–2:** Diagnostic test; set up the error log.
- **Days 3–6:** Conventions (Part 5), one rule group per day, with 15–20 practice questions daily.
- **Days 7–8:** Transitions and Rhetorical Synthesis (Part 6).
- **Days 9–11:** Your two weakest reading skills (Parts 3–4).
- **Day 12:** Second full practice test.
- **Day 13:** Error log review; redo every missed question.
- **Day 14:** Light review of the cheat sheet (Appendix D); rest.

### 6-week plan (recommended)
- **Week 1:** Diagnostic. Sentence anatomy and Boundaries.
- **Week 2:** Form, Structure, and Sense; Transitions; Rhetorical Synthesis.
- **Week 3:** Words in Context, Text Structure and Purpose, Cross-Text Connections, with vocabulary review daily.
- **Week 4:** Central Ideas, Command of Evidence (Textual and Quantitative), Inferences.
- **Week 5:** Second full practice test. Mixed timed sets, targeting error-log weaknesses.
- **Week 6:** Third full practice test early in the week, then targeted review and rest before test day.

### 12-week plan (score-maximizing)
- **Weeks 1–2:** Diagnostic; all of Part 5 (grammar), one rule group every two days.
- **Weeks 3–4:** Expression of Ideas; begin daily vocabulary.
- **Weeks 5–7:** Craft and Structure, then Information and Ideas, one skill at a time with untimed accuracy practice.
- **Week 8:** Second full practice test; rebuild the plan around the error log.
- **Weeks 9–10:** Timed mixed sets; hardest-question practice for Module 2.
- **Week 11:** Third full practice test; final error log review.
- **Week 12:** Fourth practice test early in the week (if available), light review, rest.

**A rule for every plan:** practice **accuracy before speed**. Do untimed sets until you're consistently correct on a skill, then add timing.

# Appendix C: Glossary

- **Adaptive (multistage):** the test chooses the difficulty of Module 2 based on Module 1.
- **Antecedent:** the noun a pronoun refers to.
- **Appositive:** a noun phrase that renames another noun (*my teacher, Ms. Lee,*…).
- **Comma splice:** two independent clauses joined only by a comma.
- **Conjunctive adverb:** a transition word such as *however* or *therefore*; it can't join clauses alone.
- **Dangling modifier:** an opening phrase that doesn't describe the subject right after it.
- **Dependent clause:** a clause that can't stand alone (begins with *because, although, which*, etc.).
- **Essential (restrictive) element:** information needed to identify the noun; no commas.
- **FANBOYS:** *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*, the coordinating conjunctions.
- **Finite verb:** a main verb showing tense (*discovered*, not *discovering*).
- **Fragment:** a group of words punctuated as a sentence but lacking an independent clause.
- **Independent clause:** a subject plus a finite verb that can stand alone.
- **Module:** one timed half of a section (27 questions in Reading and Writing).
- **Nonessential (nonrestrictive) element:** extra information set off by a matching pair of commas, dashes, or parentheses.
- **Pretest question:** an unscored question being tried out for future tests.
- **Run-on:** two independent clauses joined with no punctuation.

# Appendix D: One-page cheat sheet

**Every question:** read the question first → predict → eliminate by proof → choose.

**The proof rule:** if you can't point to the words (or name the rule), it isn't the answer.

**Six traps:** too extreme · out of scope · half right · true but irrelevant · opposite · recycled language.

**Words in Context:** find the clue → predict a word → watch for secondary meanings.

**Structure and purpose:** describe what each sentence *does*; check the verbs in the choices.

**Cross-text:** summarize each text's claim; Text 2 often *partly* agrees.

**Evidence:** highlight the claim → test **every part** of it.

**Graphs:** most choices are accurate; pick the one that makes *the passage's point*.

**Inference:** explain the pattern the passage sets up; stay modest.

**Joining clauses:** period · semicolon · comma + FANBOYS · colon (explanation). Never a comma alone. *However* needs a semicolon or period before it.

**Nonessential elements:** a matching pair: two commas, two dashes, or two parentheses.

**No comma** between subject and verb, verb and object, or a title and the name after it (*astronomer Vera Rubin*).

**Agreement:** cross out prepositional phrases to find the real subject.

**Finite verbs:** no main verb? Choose the tensed form, not *-ing* or *to*.

**Modifiers:** the noun after the comma must be what the opening phrase describes.

**Possessives:** one or many? Owning or not? *its* = possessive.

**Transitions:** cover the choices, name the relationship, then match.

**Notes questions:** read the **goal** first; it decides everything.

**Timing:** about 71 s per question; grammar fast, evidence slower; two-minute rule; never leave a blank.
